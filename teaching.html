Teaching

1,400+ students across 21 course offerings — databases, database system technology, software engineering, programming on the web. Led the department's migration from IBM DB2 to PostgreSQL: platform evaluation, rewriting every course material, retraining instructors, carrying students through a change none of them chose. Introduced XQuery into the curriculum from my own M.Sc. research, with assignments on real public datasets. Nominated for the U of T Scarborough Teaching Award.

“I started ‘teaching’ at an early age, when I was tutoring my sister and other kids in the neighborhood. I discovered at that early age that it is more important to guide someone through all the steps involved in solving a problem, rather than just show the final result.”

from my statement of teaching philosophy

Nine principles follow from that, each with its evidence attached. The four that shape how I run a course: teach the basics and connect the topics, because new material is a house and the rooms have to connect; teach how to think, not what to answer — “whenever possible, I choose not to give just a final answer, but rather guide the students in discovering the answer themselves”; theory as support for practice, since a career needs the foundation a few hands-on books cannot give; and improve constantly, because “it is not only the students who are meant to learn.”

In practice that meant mechanisms rather than intentions: three no-penalty grace days per course in place of a 20%-per-day late penalty; a silent period in the final 48 hours before a deadline; electronic submission, because hand-written work is unfair to whoever marks it; and reminders of the practices students most often forget, written into the assignment rather than left as their problem.

Teaching assistants were managed deliberately: large individual tasks to few TAs rather than small identical tasks spread across all of them, which keeps marking uniform and removes bottlenecks; a single tutorial room so the most experienced TA delivers each topic; and a site maintained for the TAs with the organization, guidelines and their tasks. A consistent core asked to come back year after year, which meant students got TAs who already knew the material.

Leading and managing

Teaching at this scale is a management job that happens to involve a classroom. I ran teams of up to ten teaching assistants per course, with the allocation, training and marking uniformity that implies. I supervised individual students through project and design courses at two universities — ECE496, CSC494, and graduation projects — which is one-to-one management rather than instruction. And I led the department's migration from IBM DB2 to PostgreSQL: not only the platform evaluation and the rewriting of materials, but retraining the instructors who had to deliver them and carrying several hundred students through a change none of them asked for.

A course is a product

I ran each course the way you run something people depend on. The students were its users, the term was its release cycle, and every term shipped changes nobody had asked me for.

  • Surveyed students and kept the data, rather than relying on impressions
  • Logged every bottleneck while assisting a course, then fixed them once I was the one teaching it
  • Replaced a 20%-per-day late penalty with three no-penalty grace days, and watched what changed
  • Instituted fill-in lecture handouts, written before each lecture, so the class worked the problem with me
  • Taught database pages, relational algebra and indexing with oversized Lego blocks, borrowed from my daughter
  • Moved the platform from IBM DB2 to PostgreSQL — evaluation, materials, instructor retraining, rollout
  • Built internal tooling for the teaching assistants: a site with their organization, guidelines and tasks
  • Restructured marking allocation to remove bottlenecks and keep standards uniform
  • Moved submission to electronic, and commissioned a custom-published textbook to cut student cost
  • Added office hours, discussion groups and a silent period before deadlines

None of that was in the job. It came from watching where the thing I had built was failing the people using it, and then changing it.

Sample materials

CSC343 Introduction to Databases, Assignment 3 PDF, 6 pages

Students were given 10,000 XML records of real clinical trials from clinicaltrials.gov, with the collection's DTD. Before writing a query they had to read that DTD and explore the registry's own search interface — ten of the hundred points, awarded to anyone who submitted anything, because the point was to make them look at the data. Then seven XQuery queries against a 114MB collection, each in its own file, each output in its own, run through Saxon.

Structure rules and content rules stated up front, so an answer was checkable rather than arguable. The XQuery came from my own M.Sc. research on structural and semantic query optimization; the data came from a government registry that was live while they worked on it.

3NF decomposition, worked live PDF, 4 pages

These handouts were something I introduced, and nobody asked me to. Each one is deliberately empty: a relation, its functional dependencies, then every step of the decomposition printed with its algorithm above it and the answer left blank — closures, candidate keys, minimal cover, redundant attributes on the left-hand side, redundant dependencies, the decomposition itself.

I brought them to class and we worked the problem together, me at the blackboard and the students filling in their own copies. That is the difference between watching a result appear and arriving at one. It also meant writing every handout myself, before every lecture that used one.

What I think teaching is

From the statement of teaching philosophy in my 2010 portfolio, written after a decade of it:

“I started ‘teaching’ at an early age, when I was tutoring my sister and other kids in the neighborhood. I discovered at that early age that it is more important to guide someone through all the steps involved in solving a problem, rather than just show the final result.”

Nine principles follow from that, each with the evidence for it attached — student emails, evaluation scores, the assignments themselves. The ones that shaped how I run a course:

  • Teach the basics, and connect the topics. New material is a house: without the foundation it collapses, and the rooms have to connect. Knowledge from one course should not sit isolated from the next.
  • Teach how to think, not what to answer. “Whenever possible, I choose not to give just a final answer, but rather guide the students in discovering the answer themselves: something discovered on one's own will be more easily remembered.” The same applies to exams — I want to see how a student approaches a question type they have never seen.
  • Theory as support for practice. You can become a self-taught programmer from a few hands-on books. A career in computer science needs the theory underneath it.
  • Improve constantly. “It is not only the students who are meant to learn.”

Implemented, that looked like specific mechanisms rather than intentions: three no-penalty grace days per course, replacing a 20% per-day late penalty; a silent period in the last 48 hours before a deadline; electronic submission because hand-written work is unfair to whoever marks it; and reminders of the practices students most often forget, included in the assignment itself rather than left as their problem.

Managing teaching assistants was part of the job, and I ran it deliberately: large individual tasks assigned to few TAs rather than small identical tasks spread across all of them, which keeps marking uniform and avoids bottlenecks; one tutorial room so the most experienced TA delivers each topic; and a site maintained for the TAs with the organization, guidelines and their tasks. Over the years a consistent core asked to come back, which meant the students got TAs who already knew the material.

A sample assignment

CSC343 Introduction to Databases, Winter 2009, Assignment 3. Students were given 10,000 XML files of real clinical trial records, downloaded from clinicaltrials.gov, with the collection's DTD. Before writing a single query they had to read the DTD and explore the live search interface to understand what was actually in the data — ten of the hundred points, awarded to anyone who submitted, because the point was to make them look.

Then seven XQuery queries against a 114MB collection, each in its own file, each output in its own file, run through Saxon. Structure rules and content rules stated up front, so the answer was checkable rather than arguable. The XQuery came from my own M.Sc. research on structural and semantic query optimization; the data came from a government registry that was live at the time.
